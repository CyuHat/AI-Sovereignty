The Three Levers of Control

Every AI provider wants to own three things:

The inference — where the model runs

The tool — the interface that feeds it your data

The context — your documents, your history, your defaults

Own all three, and you are the product. Own none, and you are the customer.

For the first time, you can control all three without meaningful compromise.

This is the structural frame of the entire talk. Providers like OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and XAI each want to control inference (their API), tools (Claude Code, ChatGPT app, Gemini extensions), and context/data (your chat history, uploaded files, connected services). The default is that they control all three. The thesis of this talk is that each lever is now independently controllable: you can run local inference, choose open-source or sovereign tools, and keep your context under your own governance — without accepting the performance penalties that made this impossible two years ago.

Axis 1: Distance

How far does your data physically travel?

Your laptop. A machine you own.

A server in Europe run by a company you chose.

Simple so far. Everyone in the room gets distance intuitively. Don’t linger, this axis builds trust before the harder ones.

Axis 2: Retention

What happens to your prompt after the model reads it?

Worst case: profiling. Your inputs build a longitudinal profile, combined across sessions, linked to your account.

Then: training. Your text becomes gradient updates. It stops being your data and becomes someone else’s weights.

Better: stored but unused. Logged for abuse monitoring, kept 30 days, deleted.

Best: zero data. The provider encrypts at rest with keys they don’t hold. Technically cannot comply with a data request.

Emphasize the training case: this is not storage, this is incorporation. Once your data is in the weights, deletion is mathematically impossible. There is no uninstall for training. That is why “we no longer use your chats for training” was the sentence that mattered so much in Part 0.

Axis 3: Jurisdiction

Whose laws can compel disclosure of your data?

The CLOUD Act, 2018. 18 U.S.C. § 2713. I’ll paraphrase: an American provider must hand over data, “regardless of whether such communication … is located within or outside of the United States.”

Read that again. The server’s location is legally irrelevant. Frankfurt, Dublin, Virginia: same obligation.

What counts is who controls the provider. A company incorporated in the US is reachable by US process, everywhere.

Note the direction: this law was written to help investigations. It achieves that by removing the location defense. The same reach applies to your prompts.

Technical nuance worth one sentence aloud: the CLOUD Act also has a comity procedure (18 U.S.C. § 2703(h)) allowing providers to challenge requests that conflict with foreign law. In practice, the burden is on the provider, and the customer finds out nothing. Say: “There is an escape valve. It belongs to the provider, not to you.”

So What Actually Protects You?

Legal regimes, not marketing. Two words you’ll leave with:

SecNumCloud (France, ANSSI). A certification, not a badge. Among the requirements: the provider must be majority EU-owned and controlled, with no foreign holding above the legal ceiling, EU-based staff for sensitive operations, and data staying on EU soil. Rejection is common. US parents are structurally disqualified. That’s the point.

BSI C5 (Germany, Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik). An audited cloud controls catalogue: encryption, personnel screening, where administration happens. Type 2 means the auditor checked the provider actually did the thing over time, not just wrote a policy about it.

The insight to remember: certification binds the company’s ownership, not just its promises. A German region on an American platform passes neither test. That asymmetry is the whole game.

Spoken version: “SecNumCloud doesn’t ask ‘is your datacenter in France?’ It asks ‘who owns you, who administers the machines, and under whose law can they be compelled?’ C5 asks roughly the same in German, with auditors.” If pressed on Brexit-era comparisons, stay humble: these are two national schemes, not EU-wide law. That fragmentation is itself worth a passing remark.

The Jevons Punchline

“Economists call it Jevons paradox. CFOs call it Tuesday.”

Jevons paradox: when something gets cheaper, total consumption increases.

First Question: What Do You Actually Want?

“Sovereignty” hides five different wishes. People say the word and mean very different things:

Residency. The bytes stop inside the EU.

Jurisdiction. No Washington subpoena can reach the staff who hold the keys.

Operational control. Nobody abroad can switch the service off.

Technological autonomy. The stack itself is European or open.

Retention. Nothing about you is kept, logged, or learned from.

If you cannot say which of the five you need, you cannot detect when a seller quietly swaps one for another.

Timing: 1 minute. This is the definitional slide the audience needed before the vocabulary gets weaponized by case studies later. Technological autonomy = whose code actually runs it (S3NS is French but runs Google’s stack). Retention = what providers do with your prompts afterward, ties back to the hook of the talk (training defaults from Part 0).

Sovereignty Washing

Definition: a service that solves one of the five sovereignty wishes, usually the cheapest one, and sells it as all of them.

Most common swap: residency delivered, jurisdiction implied. “Your data never leaves the EU” is technically true. The staff who control it can still be compelled from abroad.

It’s European-washing. Like greenwashing, but the thing being washed is your database. Apparently sovereignty is a color now.

Timing: 45 seconds. Deliver the joke deadpan, do not apologize for it. The definition matters more than the joke: sovereignty washing is not (always) fraud, it is an equivocation, presenting the easiest dimension (residency: servers in Germany) as if it satisfied the hardest one (jurisdiction: no compulsion by US courts). Residency is easy to buy: rent a datacenter in Frankfurt. Jurisdiction requires corporate structure: no US parent in the ownership chain, or a legal firewall like SecNumCloud’s control requirements. Operational sovereignty requires that the operator can survive losing access to US vendors (think revocable licenses, patches, control planes).

Three concrete wash patterns to name so the audience can spot them: (1) residency sold as sovereignty (hyperscaler EU regions); (2) European staffing sold as sovereignty (EU-only employees, but US-owned code and parent); (3) European legal wrapper sold as model sovereignty (routers serving US and Chinese models with an EU DPA, case study next).

For Europe, AWS chose a different structure. AWS European Sovereign Cloud GmbH, registered in Potsdam. EU-only staff. EU-citizen advisory board. Independent billing. A replica of the source code.

And wholly owned by Amazon.com Inc.

A US order to the parent reaches every subsidiary. The structure, not the network topology, decides jurisdiction.

The loophole exists. It costs something. AWS declined to pay.

The Sleeper Agent Risk

Even if the data stays in Europe, the model itself might have been trained with a hidden trigger that activates only under specific conditions.

Research from the Institute on Global Affairs (2026) documents “sleeper agents”: models trained to execute malicious behavior only under specific triggers, undetectable during standard safety training.

A model hosted in Frankfurt whose weights were trained in Beijing or San Francisco carries the alignment values of its origin — censorship patterns, refusal triggers, geopolitical bias — even when the inference is local.

Sovereignty is not just about where the bytes stop. It is about whose values the weights encode.

Sources: Hubinger et al., “Sleeper Agents” (2024); Institute on Global Affairs; PNAS Nexus / Pan & Xu

This is the model-origin dimension that residency-based sovereignty washing ignores entirely. cortecs.ai routes through Europe but serves GPT, Claude, Gemini, Qwen and DeepSeek. The data never leaves the EU, but the model’s behavior was shaped by its training jurisdiction. The Sleeper Agent research (Hubinger et al., 2024) showed backdoors persisting through safety training. Geopolitical alignment research (Pan & Xu, PNAS Nexus; CEAIS; LatticeFlow) documents systematic framing differences in Chinese-models vs Western-models on political queries. The point: data residency is necessary but not sufficient. You must also ask whose culture, whose law, and whose safety filters are baked into the weights themselves.
